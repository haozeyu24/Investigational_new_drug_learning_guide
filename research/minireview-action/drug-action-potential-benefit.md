# Connect drug action to potential benefit

*A focused mini-review of public FDA records to guide the learning guide · September 16, 2026*

**The central question is: what evidence makes it reasonable to expect this candidate to help people, and what remains an assumption?** Patient selection is one part of that argument. The FDA records also examine functional drug action, relevance of experimental systems, interpretation of biomarkers, contribution of individual mechanisms, and the limitations of extrapolating between populations.

This review examines 26 programs beyond CFTR. It is a research synthesis for deciding how to construct the lesson; it does not change the website. Most cases are small molecules. Enzyme-replacement, antibody and oligonucleotide comparators are included where they reveal a useful distinction.

## What was searched—and what was actually reviewed

The fixed search snapshot contains **1,412 extracted FDA review PDFs, representing 1,411 unique file hashes and 118,491 pages**. Automated screening for IND provenance near pharmacology, mechanism, disease-model, biological-response and selection language flagged **184 documents and 420 pages**. All 184 documents received manual triage of candidate passages and selected context: **91 were potentially substantive for this topic, 68 mainly concerned PK/safety/CMC, 19 were administrative mentions, and 6 remained uncertain**. A potentially substantive passage is not necessarily initial-IND evidence.

The **26 detailed case assessments draw on 28 FDA PDFs**, combining this screening with the earlier verified library and targeted FDA retrieval. They are not simply a subset count of the 184 flagged documents. Selected passages and their context were read closely; scanned pages and important tables were visually checked where needed.

These numbers do **not** mean 118,491 pages received full manual review. The larger retrieval census remains incomplete. Its starting index has 7,857 rows, resolving to 7,179 normalized URLs, and is not itself a census of every FDA review. Image-only pages, redactions, incomplete retrieval and confidential INDs limit coverage. A missing text match is not evidence that a document contains no relevant information.

Three source types must remain distinct:

- **Initial IND material:** a reproduced initial review, studies explicitly attributed to the initial IND, or a later review explicitly recounting the initial submission. Those are different levels of access to the original record.
- **Later IND material:** amendments, study assessments or meetings during clinical development. These illuminate how an argument develops; they are not automatically requirements before the first human dose.
- **Marketing-review context:** useful FDA assessments of mechanism or benefit whose presence in the original IND has not been established. They are labeled accordingly below.

All page references are **PDF file pages**, not necessarily the printed page numbers. The cases describe historical records, not current prescribing recommendations or current commercial status. Public approval packages also overrepresent programs that reached marketing review; this collection cannot estimate how frequently a weak rationale causes an IND to fail.

## 1. Candidate nomination does not restart the biology

The useful transition is from **evidence supporting a target or chemical series** to **an explicit argument about this candidate, this use and the next human investigation**. Existing evidence remains valuable. The work is to identify which conclusions carry over and which need a more direct test.

**Glycopyrrolate** makes this unusually clear. Its reproduced initial US IND review already described two UK studies showing bronchodilation in people with COPD. The proposed work concerned a nebulized presentation and repeated dosing. Another round of abstract muscarinic-target validation would not have addressed the central question. Initial US IND also did not mean first-ever human exposure. [FDA review, PDF 31–39](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2017/208437Orig1s000PharmR.pdf#page=31).

**Vestronidase alfa** shows a different starting point: an established enzyme deficiency. The record nevertheless separates evidence for the actual recombinant product—cellular uptake and persistence of intracellular activity—from published mouse experiments using recombinant murine enzyme. Timing and tissue access affected correction. A clear target did not guarantee that the product could reverse every disease manifestation. [IND-derived review material, PDF 13, 16–20](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2017/761047Orig1s000PharmR.pdf#page=13).

**Implication for the lesson:** start with what the team already knows. Label evidence obtained with the candidate, a tool compound, a related product, or human disease biology. Then ask which missing link could change the development plan. There is no useful universal instruction to repeat every discovery experiment after nomination. ICH M3(R2) itself describes primary pharmacodynamic studies as generally conducted during discovery. [ICH M3(R2), section 2, PDF 11](https://www.fda.gov/media/71542/download#page=11).

## 2. Knowing what the drug does is different from fully explaining why it helps

**Lumateperone** entered its reported initial IND through a receptor-occupancy study. That could measure engagement without proving a complete antipsychotic mechanism. Much later, the NDA pharmacology reviewer disputed the sponsor’s distinctive multi-transmitter account: binding, functional activity and the proposed presynaptic action did not support equally strong claims. The lesson is to keep the claim at the level supported by the experiment. [Initial IND history, PDF 34](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2019/209500Orig1s000MultidisciplineR.pdf#page=34); [later mechanistic assessment, PDF 46–49](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2019/209500Orig1s000MultidisciplineR.pdf#page=46).

**Brexanolone** is a particularly useful counterpoint to a simple genetic-repair story. Its allopregnanolone-replacement hypothesis helped focus development on postpartum depression. FDA’s later assessment explicitly notes that hormone levels did not predict the disorder, while the mechanism of clinical benefit remained unknown. Demonstrated GABA-A modulation and controlled clinical response did not require the initial replacement explanation to be complete. The public history also records the initial postpartum-depression proof-of-concept IND proceeding in 2014. [Rationale and uncertainty, PDF 19](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2019/211371Orig1s000MultidisciplineR.pdf#page=19); [IND history, PDF 27–28](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2019/211371Orig1s000MultidisciplineR.pdf#page=27).

**Minocycline foam** provides a direct later-IND example. A retained 2017 IND review states that no pharmacology studies had been conducted with the foam and that the acne mechanism and pharmacodynamics were unknown. Later NDA microbiology characterized the formulation’s antibacterial activity, while clinical trials assessed acne lesions and severity. A known antibacterial action was not treated as proof of the full causal mechanism of acne improvement. This was an established-drug formulation program, so it should not be generalized into a waiver for every novel candidate. [Later IND, PDF 140, 147, 149–150; later microbiology, PDF 133–136](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2019/212379Orig1s000MultidisciplineR.pdf#page=140).

**Implication:** an incomplete mechanism should lead to a bounded, testable explanation. It should neither be concealed by an attractive pathway diagram nor equated with absence of useful pharmacology.

## 3. A biological effect must be connected to the function that matters

**Cinacalcet’s** original US IND review connected calcium-receptor action to parathyroid-hormone suppression in animals and prior human studies. That is more informative than receptor affinity alone. But PTH lowering was still distinct from showing long-term improvement in bone disease or other patient outcomes. Calcium changes also connected the intended action with a potential adverse consequence. [Original IND reproduced in NDA, PDF 7–9, 16](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2004/21-688.pdf_Sensipar_Pharmr_P5.pdf#page=7).

**Drospirenone’s** original-IND appendix combines receptor profiling with ovulation inhibition and other functional assays. It is a useful reminder that potential benefit need not mean repairing a disease mechanism: contraception is an intended physiological intervention. Binding, downstream function and clinical effectiveness remain separate levels of evidence. [Original IND appendix, PDF 38–40](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2001/21-098_Yasmin_pharmr_P1.pdf#page=38).

**Faricimab’s** initial-IND studies asked what the second target added. A small monkey neovascularization study compared the dual VEGF-A/ANG-2 agent with other treatments, including an anti-VEGF comparator matched for binding sites. That is a functional test of the proposed advantage. It does not establish clinical superiority, pharmacological synergy or benefit in every retinal disease. [Initial-IND study summaries, PDF 18–21](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2022/761235Orig1s000PharmR.pdf#page=18).

**Implication:** the lesson should teach a sequence of claims: interaction, functional action, disease-relevant effect, potential human benefit. One successful assay should not silently stand in for all four.

Model quality also matters. In **rezafungin’s** later pre-NDA comments, FDA requested untreated-control fungal-growth information to judge the model and its activity/PK-PD interpretation. That is a concrete question about what the experiment can establish—not a request for another attractive efficacy curve. These were preliminary comments, not final meeting agreements. [IND correspondence, PDF 4–6](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2023/217417Orig1s000AdminCorres.pdf#page=4).

## 4. A biomarker needs a job description

The records contain biomarkers used to measure drug action, characterize disease, select participants, investigate response, or help choose dose. These uses are not interchangeable.

**Avacincaptad pegol’s** initial-IND study summaries distinguish C5 binding, inhibition of complement function, species differences and qualification of assays for monitoring anti-C5 activity in clinical subjects. Qualification of that pharmacodynamic assay was not validation of a pretreatment responder-selection test. Systemic complement inhibition also did not itself prove local benefit in retinal degeneration; the later geographic-atrophy rationale must not be backdated to the initial IND. [FDA review, PDF 20, 22–28](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2023/217225Orig1s000PharmR.pdf#page=23).

**Fosdenopterin** connects a defined MOCS1 defect, missing cPMP, replacement treatment and functional measurements in knockout mice. Yet the retained IND assessment reports that plasma S-sulfocysteine and urinary uric acid lacked a useful dose relationship under the tested conditions and might be inadequate for guiding therapeutic dosing. A disease-related measurable signal was not automatically a useful dosing instrument. This assessment was completed after NDA submission, reviewing studies previously submitted under IND; it was not the first-dose review. [IND assessment cover, PDF 168; pharmacology, PDF 179–180](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2021/214018Orig1s000IntegratedR.pdf#page=168).

**Implication:** whenever a marker appears, state the decision it is meant to support and what evidence shows it can support that decision. A pathway diagram does not qualify an assay.

**Eplontersen** makes the boundary especially explicit. FDA’s review quotes pre-IND advice rejecting TTR reduction as the primary efficacy endpoint of a planned Phase 3 study and recommending clinical neuropathy and quality-of-life outcomes. The advice acknowledged the importance of TTR lowering to the mechanism. This was a Phase 3 endpoint question raised at pre-IND, not a demand to prove benefit before a first human dose. [FDA review, PDF 37; IND pharmacology, PDF 127](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2024/217388Orig1s000IntegratedR.pdf#page=37).

## 5. Patient selection can begin broad, become more specific, or remain clinically defined

**Crizotinib** supplies a documented development trajectory. The FDA clinical review reconstructs an initial broad advanced-cancer Phase 1 study framed around c-Met/HGFR inhibition, followed by ALK-positive enrollment and other protocol changes. Early observations in two ALK-positive patients helped motivate enrichment; they were not by themselves validation of a predictive marker. The original program already included pharmacodynamic questions. Broad enrollment did not mean an absence of biological reasoning. [Protocol and amendment history, PDF 35–38](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2011/202570Orig1s000MedR.pdf#page=35).

**Ensartinib** is even closer to the initial planning question. Later IND correspondence recounts pre-IND advice to specify enrollment numbers for patients negative for both proposed ALK/cMET alterations and to identify potential pharmacodynamic endpoints. The first study included broad advanced-solid-tumor dose escalation, with ALK-positive NSCLC expansion. This is evidence of a deliberate plan to learn around a hypothesis, not a universal FDA instruction to include marker-negative patients. [IND correspondence and initial-study history, PDF 49–50](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2025/218171Orig1s000AdminCorres.pdf#page=49).

**Ruxolitinib** illustrates a different outcome. In its 2011 marketing review, FDA’s genomics assessment found benefit in both JAK2V617F-positive and negative groups and did not recommend pretreatment mutation determination. That is later clinical evidence, not an initial IND exemption. It shows why a mutation involved in disease biology need not become a mandatory treatment-selection gate. [FDA genomics assessment, PDF 466–467](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2024/202192Orig1s000.pdf#page=466).

**Tazemetostat’s** actual 2018 IND meeting minutes expose a subtler problem: an assay detecting seven EZH2 variants could classify tumors with other alterations, including copy-number gains, as “wild type.” FDA asked for better characterization. A test-negative group is not necessarily a biologically unaltered group. [End-of-Phase 2 minutes, PDF 33–38](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2020/213400Orig1s000AdminCorres.pdf#page=33).

**Implication:** patient selection belongs in this lesson, but as one consequence of the action-to-benefit argument. A disease diagnosis, treatment setting, physiological state or prior therapy can make a population coherent without a predictive molecular marker. Conversely, an attractive genetic hypothesis still needs a suitable assay and supporting response evidence.

## 6. Shared mechanism does not settle disease stage, treatment setting or clinical benefit

In **vorasidenib’s** actual 2019 IND meeting, the sponsor distinguished activity in enhancing and non-enhancing glioma and described a perioperative study measuring intratumoral 2-HG. FDA separately questioned the clinical meaningfulness of the proposed progression-free-survival improvement and encouraged collection of evidence closer to patient benefit. Even an IDH-mutant population did not make disease stage, pathway modulation and meaningful benefit equivalent. These were later-development decisions, not universal initial-IND prerequisites. [Meeting minutes, PDF 39–42, 47](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2024/218784Orig1s000AdminCorres.pdf#page=39).

**Zuranolone’s** record describes a proposed bridge from major depressive disorder studies to postpartum depression that became more demanding during development. Shared symptoms and pharmacology were insufficient to assume transferable response; FDA later asked for additional population-specific evidence. [IND-development history, PDF 150–154](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2023/217369Orig2s000IntegratedR.pdf#page=150).

**Auvelity** shows that the decisive experiment can concern the treatment itself. Later FDA discussions considered what dextromethorphan added to bupropion at the exposure produced by their combination. Rapid metabolism of dextromethorphan alone complicated the apparently simple component comparison. The exact antidepressant mechanism remained unresolved. [End-of-Phase 2 history, PDF 31–32; pharmacology, PDF 69](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2023/215430Orig1s000MultidisciplineR.pdf#page=31).

**Implication:** a strong development plan tests the weakest consequential inference. Sometimes that is mechanism; sometimes model relevance, population, comparator, or the meaning of the response measure.

## What FDA requires—and the scientific judgment the sponsor still needs

The regulation calls for a study rationale, intended indications, an investigational plan, participant selection and exclusion criteria, and sufficient pharmacology/toxicology information to support the safety of the proposed investigation. The pharmacology section describes drug effects, mechanisms and disposition **if known**. These requirements do not amount to a universal demand for a validated predictive biomarker or a complete molecular explanation before an initial IND. [21 CFR 312.23(a)(3), (6), (8)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-312/subpart-B/section-312.23).

FDA’s Phase 1 IND guidance explicitly permits unknown pharmacology information to be identified as unknown. It says missing potential-effectiveness information generally should not, by itself, cause a Phase 1 clinical hold, while recognizing that pharmacology studies may be necessary to address safety or interpret toxicology. That is a distinction about regulatory purpose, not advice to proceed without a credible scientific rationale. [FDA Phase 1 guidance, PDF 12](https://www.fda.gov/media/71203/download#page=12).

Context matters. ICH S9, for its defined advanced-cancer setting, recommends preliminary characterization of mechanism, schedule dependence and antitumor activity before Phase 1. It also says the models need not use the same tumor types intended for clinical evaluation. That is more specific than either “prove the whole mechanism” or “animal efficacy does not matter.” [ICH S9, section 2.1, PDF 6](https://www.fda.gov/media/73161/download#page=6).

My scientific recommendation for the sponsor is therefore to make four things explicit:

1. **The claim:** the candidate is expected to produce a particular useful effect in a stated clinical context.
2. **The support:** which observations establish candidate action, disease relevance and plausibility in humans.
3. **The uncertainty:** what is inferred, what could alternatively explain the findings, and where tool or model evidence might not transfer.
4. **The next decision:** what result would strengthen, narrow, redirect or stop the proposed path.

Those four items are a teaching framework derived from these cases, not four FDA-defined submission requirements.

## How I would use this to construct the lesson

Keep **Connect drug action to potential benefit**. Its output should be a defensible candidate-specific benefit argument, with the important uncertainties visible. It should not become a second target-validation chapter or primarily a patient-selection tutorial.

Use one horizontal sequence, with the existing evidence shown beneath each stage:

**What does this candidate do? → Why might that help? → Where should that reasoning hold? → What could we learn in people?**

The first stage distinguishes binding from function and candidate evidence from supporting tools. The second connects the effect to a useful physiological or disease outcome. The third addresses model limitations, tissue access, disease setting and potential population differences. The last identifies a measurable human question; detailed dose modeling belongs in the next PK/PD lesson, and protocol implementation belongs in clinical planning.

For the situation you described—an excellent candidate active in three cell lines from different tumors—the lesson should help the learner ask: do the lines share a relevant dependency; does the candidate produce the intended action at the active concentrations; what competing explanation fits; and which experiment would most improve the rationale for choosing a clinical setting? The answer might be a functional biomarker, a broader disease hypothesis, or evidence that the current rationale is still weak. Three responsive lines do not force a genetic selection strategy, and they do not by themselves define a patient population. This is an application of the framework, not a case reported in the reviewed FDA documents.

For examples, I would use a small contrasting set rather than one supposedly universal program:

| Example | What it teaches | Best placement |
|---|---|---|
| Cinacalcet | Candidate action, measurable physiological response and eventual benefit are separate | First, an intuitive action-to-function example |
| Brexanolone or minocycline foam | A benefit mechanism can remain incomplete; the uncertainty must be stated precisely | A contrasting route to a testable rationale |
| Crizotinib | Population hypotheses can develop through evidence rather than arrive fully formed | How the rationale becomes more specific |
| Fosdenopterin or avacincaptad pegol | A marker that measures biology does not automatically support every decision | Distinguishing measurement from prediction |
| Vorasidenib | Target modulation and disease genetics still leave questions about meaningful benefit | A clearly labeled look ahead to clinical learning |

Keep detailed FDA cases under **See examples**. Under **Understand**, teach the reasoning and distinguish regulatory expectations from the sponsor’s scientific choices. The most useful recurring question is: **What does this result allow us to claim—and what does it leave open?**
