# Define the drug people will receive

A drug name identifies an active substance. It does not, by itself, identify the material that made a toxicology study informative or the preparation that a participant will receive. The public FDA record repeatedly turns on that distinction. A vehicle can change the interpretation of animal toxicity; a salt change can require an exposure bridge; a valve can reopen a clinical comparison after Phase 3. The useful question for this lesson is therefore: **what must be true of this particular preparation for the evidence we already have to apply?**

This focused review examines eleven development programs, predominantly small molecules, with two antibody comparators. It separates contemporaneous original IND passages, retrospective accounts of early decisions, later IND meetings, and marketing-stage consequences. It is a purposive synthesis of verified public passages, not an estimate of how often FDA raises each issue. Quantitative formulations and manufacturing details are frequently redacted. Those gaps limit the reconstruction of a product, but often leave the decision and its scientific rationale visible.

## Start with the administered product, then state the bridge

The initial glycopyrrolate inhalation review is an unusually concrete starting point. It identifies glycopyrrolate alongside sodium chloride, citric acid, pH-adjusting ingredients and water for injection; much of the quantitative table and device description is withheld. During the initial 30-day review, FDA asked for assay and impurity profiles before and after nebulization. The source therefore supports teaching the relationship between formulation and administration, without inventing the hidden recipe. The clinical material was a preparation used through a delivery system, and its relevant characteristics had to survive that use. [Glycopyrrolate, original IND review and memo, PDF 36–37, 70](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2017/208437Orig1s000PharmR.pdf#page=36).

Our inference is that a useful product definition has two layers: a description of what is supplied, and a description of what is actually administered. The first includes substance form, composition, strength, presentation and route. The second includes preparation and delivery. Neither should be mistaken for a complete commercial specification. The immediate output is a traceable definition of the material for the proposed study, including any reasonable alternatives still being compared.

Ivacaftor provides a positive example of bridging different preparations. An original IND review reproduced in the NDA reports comparable dog pharmacokinetics for clinical-associated and nonclinical-associated materials using the stated PEG/PVP/KOAc and SLS/methylcellulose/water vehicles. Important material identifiers are redacted; the visible vehicles are not a full clinical recipe. This is evidence directed at a specific uncertainty: whether the different preparations produced comparable systemic exposure. It does not prove every attribute identical or establish local tolerability by itself. The case teaches that a bridge is an argument about consequences, supported by a relevant comparison. [Ivacaftor, original IND 74633, PDF 79, 90](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2012/203188Orig1s000PharmR.pdf#page=90).

Suvorexant shows what remains when that argument is less secure. FDA considered the initial one-month rat and dog studies adequate for the proposed first trial, yet the June 2008 May Proceed letter questioned a nonclinical excipient absent from the clinical formulation because it could affect animal safety and relevance to humans. Later, the sponsor elected to conduct two toxicology batteries rather than provide the requested justification for vehicle use. The source supports an unresolved bridge alongside permission for a limited trial; it does not establish a universal requirement to repeat all toxicology. The excipient identities remain redacted. [Suvorexant, NDA account of initial and later IND decisions, PDF 34](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2014/204569Orig1s000PharmR.pdf#page=34).

These cases belong together in the lesson. Comparable exposure is one possible answer. A bounded permission with a remaining question is another. Learners should identify what evidence would change the bridge assessment, rather than equating “may proceed” with a permanent endorsement of every material choice.

## Ingredients have functions, and prior knowledge has boundaries

Pennsaid makes the word “inactive” scientifically consequential. The NDA's account of the initial IND describes a hold recommendation for missing pertinent nonclinical evidence. FDA rejected reliance on published articles alone and questioned the proposal to treat DMSO as inactive because of its anti-inflammatory activity. The requested work addressed its contribution, skin penetration and interactions with diclofenac. Later discussions accepted specified human and skin evidence in place of some initially recommended animal work, and trials were allowed to proceed. This is a historical product-specific sequence, not a contemporary rule that every DMSO formulation needs the same studies. [Pennsaid, initial IND and 1993–1995 history, PDF 45–46](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2009/020947s000pharmr.pdf#page=45).

The teaching implication is to ask what each component does before assigning it to a regulatory column. A penetration modifier can affect exposure. A vehicle can affect interpretation of toxicity. Prior use can be valuable evidence, but its relevance depends on amount, route, contact site and the new preparation. The lesson should make students write these links explicitly; an ingredient list alone will not reveal them.

Halobetasol lotion provides a useful counterweight to an ever-expanding demand for new studies. A December 2008 original-submission memo accepted prior topical experience, irritation results and wavelength scans as support for a single-dose vasoconstrictor study in healthy volunteers. The sponsor intended to select one formulation using that study. FDA separately addressed the bridge to an approved formulation, local safety and later marketing needs. Product definition can therefore be provisional while remaining adequate for a precisely bounded experiment. The permission was not approval of every candidate formulation for unrestricted treatment. [Halobetasol, contemporaneous original IND memo, PDF 103–105](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2015/208183Orig1s000Admincorres.pdf#page=103).

Minocycline foam shows another boundary: the population. The pre-IND discussion addressed a clinical bridge from a topical product to systemic nonclinical findings for oral Solodyn, alongside dermal safety. After an adult PK protocol opened the IND, FDA requested pediatric PK. At the end-of-Phase-2 meeting, adult data alone were not considered enough; pediatric maximal-use conditions also needed evaluation. The point is not that topical products always need this exact study. It is that a bridge must cover the intended users and exposure conditions, and systemic reliance does not answer local safety. [Minocycline, pre-IND, initial IND and EOP2 history, PDF 27](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2019/212379Orig1s000MultidisciplineR.pdf#page=27).

## Changes create questions that can be answered in different ways

Baricitinib's phosphate-to-free-base change is an instructive later IND example. Earlier pharmacology, ADME and general toxicology had used the phosphate salt. When the sponsor proposed free base for carcinogenicity testing after changing clinical development, FDA initially requested the phosphate form or more systemic exposure bridging. The sponsor supplied additional rationale, and the existing bridge was subsequently accepted. The visible sequence is a change in the interpretation of available evidence, not necessarily the generation of another study. Underlying exposure data are not reproduced in this passage. [Baricitinib, 2011–2012 IND history, PDF 53](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2018/207924Orig1s000PharmR.pdf#page=53).

Atrovent HFA illustrates why presentation belongs in product identity. After Phase 3, a third-generation valve prompted additional PK and dose-ranging studies. FDA also requested delivery information, particle-size distribution and plume geometry. The study material and proposed market presentation could not be connected simply by naming ipratropium. This was a later development issue; the separate 1994 initial hold concerned missing Phase 1 data and should not be attributed to the later valve change. [Atrovent, development history, PDF 38–39](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2004/021527s000_MedR.pdf#page=38).

The ACER-001 sodium phenylbutyrate pre-NDA meeting adds the patient's dose to this definition. FDA linked a taste-mask coating to dissolution under differing pH conditions and asked about fed-state differences between children and adults. It also questioned how the proposed pouch sizes could deliver doses outside particular weight or body-surface-area ranges. A formulation can be chemically defined yet fail to provide a feasible presentation for part of the intended population. The relevant ranges and some coating details are redacted; these are late IND questions about the future NDA, not original IND instructions. [ACER-001, April 2021 pre-NDA meeting under IND 143822, PDF 6–8](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2023/214860Orig1s000AdminCorres.pdf#page=6).

## Antibody comparators make the same question harder

Faricimab demonstrates a selective response to formulation changes. In a 2017 Type C interaction, FDA agreed that no additional nonclinical studies were needed to qualify 7 mM methionine, while requesting context for retinal degeneration in control animals. It also accepted an explanation for a buffer change to histidine acetate and pH 5.5. The lesson is that evidence, context and rationale can resolve a formulation question without automatically generating another animal study. This was later IND development, not the original 2013 submission. [Faricimab, BLA account of the 2017 meeting, PDF 15–16](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2022/761235Orig1s000PharmR.pdf#page=15).

Teplizumab shows the possible clinical consequence of an inadequate material bridge. In 2018 IND correspondence, FDA rejected an outline of a manufacturing-transfer comparability plan and requested a detailed protocol. A later BLA history records lack of PK comparability between the pivotal-trial and planned commercial products in the 2021 complete response; additional PK/PD evidence and a higher dose of the AGC material subsequently supported exposure matching. These are connected observations, but the public passages do not prove that the earlier outline alone caused the later outcome. Antibody comparability must remain explicitly labeled as a comparator to small-molecule development. [Teplizumab, 2018 minutes, PDF 312–313](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2026/761183Orig1s010.pdf#page=312); [later BLA history, PDF 136](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2026/761183Orig1s010.pdf#page=136).

## Regulatory floor and lesson construction

The regulation requires phase-appropriate CMC information supporting identity, strength, quality and purity. FDA's Phase 1 submission guidance specifically asks sponsors to explain differences between clinical and toxicology products and their possible safety implications. It anticipates limited early information and subsequent development. Those requirements and recommendations provide the framework; choosing which comparison resolves a particular formulation uncertainty is a scientific judgment. [21 CFR 312.23(a)(7)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-312/subpart-B/section-312.23); [Phase 1 IND guidance, PDF 6–7](https://www.fda.gov/media/71203/download#page=6).

The lesson should produce a **material bridge**, not a list of CMC vocabulary. First, the learner defines supplied and administered preparations for one proposed study. Second, they compare that preparation with the materials underlying toxicology, prior human experience and any reference product. Third, they assign each difference a plausible consequence, an existing line of evidence and a remaining decision. A side-by-side table is useful precisely because “same active ingredient” cannot fill every cell.

The handoff to nonclinical development is a statement of which test articles and exposures support the proposed material, with local effects kept visible. The handoff to clinical planning is a feasible presentation, preparation and dosing scheme for the intended participants. Process controls then address reproducibility of that defined material; stability addresses its suitability over time and through administration. These three questions connect, but keeping them distinct prevents a single acceptable assay or familiar drug name from silently carrying the entire argument.
